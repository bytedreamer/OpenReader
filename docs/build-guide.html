What you are building

A PD — Peripheral Device, in OSDP's vocabulary. It is the reader at the door. It sits on an RS-485 bus, answers polls from an ACU (Access Control Unit, the panel), and when someone presents a card it reports the credential on the next poll.

The panel decides whether the door opens. The reader never does. That split is the whole point of OSDP, and it is why a reader is a comparatively simple device to build.

Which end do you read from?

The tolerance band — gold or silver on most parts — goes on the right. When both ends look plausible, the wider gap sits between the multiplier and the tolerance band. Still ambiguous? Measure it. A resistor isn't directional, so guessing wrong about the reading direction costs nothing.

On a 5-band part the third band is a digit, not the multiplier. Reading a 1% part as though it were 5% is the classic way to end up with a resistor 10× off.

R marks a decimal point — 4R7 is 4.7 Ω, and 000 or 0R0 is a zero-ohm jumper. The trap in the 4-digit scheme is the final digit: 1001 is 1 kΩ but 1002 is 10 kΩ, and on an 0603 part that difference is a fraction of a millimetre of ink.

Verify with a meter regardless

Expect 990–1010 Ω for a 1 kΩ 1% part, or 950–1050 Ω at 5%. Measure with the resistor out of circuit and touching nothing else — one connected end is enough to shunt the reading through whatever it's attached to, and your own fingers across the probes will read low on high-value parts.

02

The one mistake that kills the board

Read before connecting anything

The SH-U12 is a 5 V board. The ESP32-C6 is not 5 V tolerant.

The SH-U12's TXD pin — the transceiver's output, the one carrying data from the bus to your microcontroller — idles and swings at 5 V. The ESP32-C6's GPIOs are rated to 3.3 V with no tolerance for more. Wiring TXD straight to a GPIO will damage the pin, and often the chip.

The other direction is fine. The MAX13487's input threshold is 2.0 V on a 5 V supply, so the ESP32-C6's 3.3 V output drives it with margin. Only the TXD → RX path needs shifting.

A tempting shortcut is to power the SH-U12 from 3.3 V instead. Don't. The MAX13487 is specified for 4.5–5.5 V; below that, the differential voltage it puts on the bus falls out of spec. You get a reader that works on the bench with 30 cm of wire and fails on 200 m of real cable — the worst kind of bug to chase.

The level shifter

A resistor divider is the right tool. One direction only, low data rate, nothing to go wrong.

Keep both resistors close to the ESP32-C6 end and keep the wire after them short — a divider raises source impedance, so a long run past it picks up noise more easily. At OSDP baud rates this is undemanding; 10 cm of jumper wire is fine.

If you would rather use a part than two resistors

A 74AHCT1G125 or 74LVC1G17 running from 3.3 V works and is faster, and a TXS0108E breakout does the job with more pins than you need. All of them are more parts for the same result at 9600 baud.

03

Wiring

The onboard peripherals already claim GPIO 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 14, 15, 21 and 22. GPIO 12/13 are the native USB pins and GPIO 9 is the BOOT strap, so those are off limits too. Everything below is chosen from what remains. Strapping pins on the ESP32-C6 are GPIO 4, 5, 8, 9 and 15 — nothing here uses one.

Why not the pins labelled TX and RX?

The header's TX/RX are GPIO16/GPIO17 — UART0's default mapping, which is what the silkscreen is naming. They really are free: this board has no USB-UART bridge chip, so flashing and the console both ride the ESP32-C6's native USB on GPIO12/13, and nothing is holding UART0.

We still don't put RS-485 there. The ROM bootloader transmits its banner on GPIO16 at 115200 baud on every reset, before any firmware runs. On a bench that is harmless noise the panel drops on a CRC check; on a live multi-drop bus it is garbage arriving on top of another device's reply.

That behaviour belongs to the pin, not the UART peripheral — the ESP32-C6 routes any UART to any GPIO through its GPIO matrix, so assigning UART1 to GPIO16/17 would not avoid it, because the ROM has already transmitted by then. You can suppress ROM printing (GPIO15 is the strapping pin for it, and there is an eFuse) but that is a permanent change to make for tidier wiring.

Left alone, TX/RX are a debug console you can attach while the RS-485 side is busy talking to a panel — worth more than the neater cable run, especially while bringing up Secure Channel.

The RC522's SDA pin is a chip select, not I²C data. The silkscreen is misleading and it catches almost everyone once.

The RC522 gets header pins of its own. It cannot share the LCD's bus: that bus's clock and data (GPIO6/7) never leave the board. Its lines are picked from the free header GPIOs, avoiding the strapping pins.

Wire it once and it works either way the firmware chooses to clock it. The default build bit-bangs these pins in software, which leaves the chip's only hardware SPI master free for the LCD — so the display and the card reader run at the same time. A build without the display can drive the same pins from hardware SPI2 instead. Same wiring, same table.

Keep the leads short. Neither bus is fussy — hardware SPI2 runs them at 5 MHz and the bit-banged bus at roughly 500 kHz, both far under the MFRC522's 10 MHz ceiling — but long unshielded jumpers with no solid ground return between the boards will misread before either rate does.

Audible output — active buzzer (optional)

Only worth wiring if you want the reader to answer osdp_BUZ. Everything above works without it. A three-pin active buzzer module is the part.

It must be an active buzzer

The firmware drives the pin to a static high or low and never generates a waveform. An active module has its own oscillator and beeps; a passive one gives you a single click and then silence. Listings are often ambiguous, or sell mixed packs of both. The test takes a second: put 3.3 V straight across the buzzer, and a continuous tone means active.

Then turn it on, because it is off by default: idf.py menuconfig → OpenReader → Audible output. Off is the honest default rather than a cautious one — enabling it also makes osdp_CAP report an audible output, and an ACU told a reader can beep will command beeps and has no way to discover they never happened. Claim the sounder when the sounder exists.

If it shrieks continuously from boot and goes quiet only when the ACU commands a beep, the module is active low; there is a second option directly beneath the first for that. The firmware drives the pin to its silent level before anything else runs, so a correctly configured board is quiet from power-up.

No beep logic is needed. The PD library decodes osdp_BUZ and resolves its on/off/count pattern over time, calling the firmware on each edge — the reader just switches the pin. It is also silenced whenever the OSDP link drops, because osdp_BUZ can command a continuous pattern and a reader that lost comms mid-beep would otherwise sound until someone unplugged it.

Enclosure tamper switch (optional)

Only worth wiring once the reader is in a box. Everything else works without it, and the option is off by default — with it on and no switch fitted, the input's pull-up reads the floating pin as a permanent tamper.

Enclosure tamper switch (optional)
SwitchESP32-C6Note
One legGPIO4 (header GP4)
Other legGNDNo resistor — the internal pull-up supplies the high level

Use a normally-closed switch — one the enclosure lid holds shut. The lid then holds GP4 low, opening the lid releases it high, and so does a cut, corroded or disconnected wire. Sabotage reads as tamper. A normally-open switch cannot tell an intact quiet loop from a snipped one, so anyone with wire cutters defeats it silently. If yours is normally-open, there is an option for it directly beneath the first.

GP4 and GP5 are the microSD slot's chip select and data-out on this carrier, so using them for these two parts gives the card slot up — nothing in the firmware has ever used it. They are also strapping pins, which here does not matter: on the ESP32-C6, GP8/GP9 set the boot mode and GP15 the JTAG source, but GP4 and GP5 select SDIO clock edges, and this firmware never uses SDIO. Both float by default with no internal pull resistor. The build still rejects a tamper pin on 8, 9 or 15.

GP9 has a second job, and it needs no wiring: it is the BOOT button, and the firmware reads it as an ordinary input to offer the physical Secure Channel key reset — hold it ten seconds while the reader is running and the stored key is erased. Nothing about the button’s original behaviour changes. The ROM samples that pin at reset and never again, so holding BOOT across a reset still gets download mode with the key untouched; the firmware’s read happens long afterwards. Do not route anything else to GP9 — the build rejects an RS-485 pin there for this reason.

No reporting logic is needed either. The switch is debounced over 50 ms and its state goes into the tamper byte of osdp_LSTATR, both when the ACU asks with osdp_LSTAT and unsolicited on the next poll after a change. The 50 ms is not about contact bounce, which settles far faster — it stops a marginal switch, or a door that shakes in its frame, from turning into a stream of status reports at the head end.

Not the GRB nearly every WS2812 datasheet specifies. Measured on the bench: with the strip declared GRB, an ACU commanding green lit the pixel red. The led_strip component offers only GRB and GRBW, so the firmware corrects for it in one place — paint() in main/status_led.c hands the driver red and green swapped.

The tell is that red and green trade places, and so do cyan and magenta, while blue, white and off look right either way. A quick sweep through the colours can therefore appear to pass. If a board revision ever arrives with the orthodox order, that one line is what changes.

04

The bus itself

Three things matter. Skipping any of them produces a reader that works across a desk and fails down a corridor.

Twisted pair

A and B must be the two conductors of one twisted pair. That twist is what makes RS-485 reject noise; using two random wires from a ribbon cable throws the entire benefit away.

Ground

Connect the bus's signal ground. RS-485 is differential but not magic — the receivers still need both ends to sit inside a common-mode range, −7 V to +12 V for this part. Two devices on separate supplies with no ground reference can drift outside it and stop communicating, or damage something. On shielded cable, ground the shield at one end only.

Termination

120 Ω across A and B at each of the two physical ends of the bus, and only there. A reader in the middle of a daisy chain gets no terminator.

Check whether your SH-U12 already has one fitted — look for a resistor marked 121 or 120 near the A/B terminals. If it does and your reader sits mid-bus, remove it, or the bus is over-terminated and the signal collapses.

What you don't need

No external bias network. The MAX13487E has a true failsafe receiver, so an idle undriven bus reads as a logical high — which is the UART's idle state. Nothing appears on the line until someone actually transmits.

Power

On the bench, the USB-C cable powers everything: the board regulates 3.3 V for itself and the RC522, and passes 5 V through for the SH-U12. Draw is modest — roughly 30–80 mA for the ESP32-C6 with Wi-Fi idle, plus about 25 mA for the RC522 with its antenna on.

For an installed reader, take the 12 V that panels normally distribute and buck it to 5 V at the reader. Do not run 5 V down a long cable from the panel; the drop over a couple of hundred metres of 24 AWG will put you below what the regulator needs.

05

Bring-up, in order

Do these in sequence. Each one rules out a layer, so a failure tells you where to look instead of leaving you guessing.

5.1Board alive

Flash with nothing but USB connected:

idf.py set-target esp32c6
idf.py build flash monitor

The RGB LED should breathe blue — the firmware saying no ACU is polling me, which is correct, because none is.

I (312) main: OpenReader starting
I (318) led: WS2812 on GPIO8
I (330) rs485: UART1 up at 9600 baud (TX=0 RX=1)

5.2The RC522 answers

Same run, a line or two later:

I (352) rc522: MFRC522 online, VersionReg=0x92

0x91 and 0x92 are the genuine v1.0 and v2.0 parts; clones report other values and work fine. What you must not see:

E (352) rc522: no response (VersionReg=0x00) — check SPI wiring ...

0x00 or 0xFF means the SPI bus is not talking at all — MISO stuck low or high. In order of likelihood: SDA/CS on the wrong pin, MISO and MOSI swapped, or the module powered from 5 V.

Now present the card from the kit:

I (5012) main: card A3:7F:2C:19 (SAK 0x08)

If the UID reads only intermittently, the antenna is marginal — usually long jumper wires to the RC522, or the module lying against something metallic.

5.3Does your transceiver echo?

Some auto-direction transceivers leave the receiver enabled while driving the line, so every byte you transmit arrives straight back on RX.

This is not fatal either way. The OSDP library filters frames by direction, so it recognises its own replies as replies and drops them. Echo costs some wasted buffer churn, nothing more.

But if you would rather not have it, find out which kind of board you have. With the SH-U12 wired and A/B going nowhere, add this to app_main() temporarily:

const char *probe = "ECHO?";
uart_write_bytes(BOARD_RS485_UART, probe, strlen(probe));
uart_wait_tx_done(BOARD_RS485_UART, pdMS_TO_TICKS(100));
uint8_t back[16];
int n = uart_read_bytes(BOARD_RS485_UART, back, sizeof(back),
                        pdMS_TO_TICKS(50));
ESP_LOGI("probe", "got %d bytes back", n);

got 5 bytes back means your board echoes. got 0 bytes back means it does not. To suppress an echo you actually measured:

idf.py menuconfig
  → OpenReader
    → [*] Discard local echo of transmitted bytes
Only if the probe showed an echo

If the board does not echo and suppression is on, it will eat the first bytes of every command the panel sends, and your reader will never answer a poll.

5.4The panel polls you

Connect A, B and ground. Set the panel to poll address 0 at 9600 baud — or change the reader to match, under menuconfig → OpenReader → OSDP PD address.

The RGB LED stops breathing blue the moment the first poll is answered:

I (18420) osdp: link online

No panel yet? The OSDP-Embedded repo ships osdp-acu-mock, which drives a PD from a PC serial port — the fastest way to test a reader without a panel on the desk.

Then present a card. The panel should report the credential.

07

Beyond the bench

Secure Channel

Built and on by default: SC1 with AES-128 from mbedTLS on the chip’s own accelerator, and the handshake nonce from the hardware RNG.

The reader ships in install mode, answering the handshake on SCBK-D — the key from spec D.4 that every OSDP implementation knows — so a panel can reach a fresh reader and give it a real one with osdp_KEYSET. That is not security; it is the state in which security gets configured, which is why the LCD says INSTALL in amber rather than SECURE in green, and keeps saying it even once a session is up.

Two things to do before you trust it with a door. Key it from the panel, so it stops answering a published key — that is one-way, and the way back is holding BOOT for ten seconds. And burn an eFuse HMAC block first, so the key the panel gives it is wrapped to this chip instead of sitting in readable flash; without one, every boot says so at error level. Both are covered in the README’s “Secure Channel” section.

Enclosure and tamper

Supported — a switch on GP4, see step 03 — but off by default and unwired on a bench build, in which case the reader still answers “normal” to every osdp_LSTAT. A reader in a wall box should have the switch fitted and the option turned on. The panel asks; give it a true answer.

LED brightness

LED_LEVEL in main/status_led.c is at full scale (255) — what a demo on a desk wants, and more than a reader at a door does, since the WS2812 is genuinely unpleasant close up at that level. Turn it down for an installed unit. Every colour is mixed proportionally to it, so amber stays amber and the offline breath keeps its ramp wherever it is set.

The credential itself

A MIFARE Classic UID is not a secret. It is readable by anyone with a phone and clonable with commodity hardware. Deciding whether a UID is sufficient authentication for a given door belongs to the access control system, not to the reader — but when it isn't sufficient, this reader has an answer.

It reads PKOC as well. The card holds a P-256 key pair in its secure element, its public key is the credential, and at the door it proves it holds the matching private key by signing a nonce the reader supplies. Cloning one needs the private key rather than the bits it broadcasts. No extra hardware is involved — the same RC522 carries it, over ISO/IEC 14443-4 — so this is a menuconfig decision and a supply of PKOC cards, not another shopping list. See the README’s “PKOC” section.

Two settings there deserve a decision rather than a default. Report the UID when a card has no PKOC applet is on, which keeps a mixed population of badges working; turn it off for a PKOC-only door, because a panel that accepts either is only as strong as the weaker. And verify the card’s signature must stay on for anything with a door behind it — with it off the reader repeats whatever public key a card claims, which is worth exactly as much as a UID.
